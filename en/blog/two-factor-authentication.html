What is two-factor authentication?

Two-factor authentication, commonly called 2FA or two-step verification, is the protection of a personal account by a second identification step, most often a code called an OTP, sent by SMS or produced by a security token, with maximum priority and valid for a few minutes.

Two-factor authentication differs from multi-factor authentication in the number of elements used. With MFA (Multi-factor authentication), all three factors are used.

What are the authentication factors?

Multi-factor authentication rests on three distinct proofs of identity, each matching one validation step. Two-factor authentication combines two of them, MFA uses all three. What makes it solid is the nature of the proofs, not how many there are. These are the three families of factors, as you meet them on an online account:

  • Something you know, such as a code
  • Something you have, such as a card or a mobile phone
  • Something you are, such as a fingerprint, or facial or voice recognition.

In practice, on an online account, identifying yourself with a username and a password is the first factor. The second is the smartphone in your possession, to which an SMS OTP is sent: a text message containing a code to type, which proves that the phone is indeed yours.

2FA use cases

While this method is well known for validating an online payment (still in use in Europe until the PSD2 regulation came into force), it is becoming widespread for other applications:

  • Access to a professional account
  • A cryptocurrency wallet
  • A merchant account holding sensitive information
  • Resetting a forgotten password.

A security barrier

Above all, it is a genuine additional security barrier for your professional online services.

A simple password is no longer enough. Access to sensitive data by malicious individuals can be devastating, and most companies are no better protected against cyberattacks than private accounts.

A corporate email account can be compromised by phishing or identity theft and become an entry point to steal strategic information, or even money. If a password reset is possible simply by receiving an email, access to your platform is child's play.

Passwords already used elsewhere and made public can be used to access a professional account. 73% of passwords are used for more than one account, which is as many chances of a successful hack.
