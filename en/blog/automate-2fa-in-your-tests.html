Automating two-factor authentication in your tests

Two-factor authentication is the last manual step in many test pipelines. This guide covers the whole subject: what exactly blocks, the four families of second factor, what a browser can reach and where it stops, and the three possible approaches to clearing the step.

By Sylvain Perez, creator of Q-Bot. Updated 27 August 2026

The starting point

What exactly blocks in a test pipeline?

An automated scenario can fill in a username and a password. It cannot approve a request that lands on a phone. The run therefore stops at the front door, and everything past it stays untested, however good the rest of the suite may be.

The symptom shows up in a run report: a large number of failures, all in the same place, with the same trace. The suite is not flaky; it simply cannot clear one step.

What that costs is measured in hours, and it is the subject of the guide on the cost of the manual step. The symptom itself is covered in « why your night runs stop at login ».

The landscape

What are the four families of second factor?

A computed code, a request to approve, a code shown nowhere else, and a QR code to scan. These four families are not automated the same way, and that distinction is the only one that matters when you choose an approach. Everything else in this guide follows from it.

In order of increasing difficulty:

  • a computed code (TOTP): Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator. It derives from a shared secret and the current time, per RFC 6238
  • a request to approve: LuxTrust Mobile, itsme. No code, just a gesture on the enrolled device
  • a displayed code, readable only on the app's own screen
  • a QR code shown on a screen that the device has to scan

The first family can be computed. The other three cannot, and that is the whole difference.

The tools

What can a browser reach, and where does it stop?

Selenium, Cypress and Playwright drive the rendered document, and they do it very well. They have no hold on a system notification or a native app: no selector reaches a phone's screen. That is not a weakness, it is their scope.

Selenium, Cypress and Playwright act on the DOM. As soon as the step leaves the browser they are outside their domain, and no option brings them back in.

Selenium's documentation says so itself and advises disabling two-factor authentication in testing. That is a defensible recommendation, and the dedicated guide sets out where it applies and where it costs you.

The three routes

What are the three possible approaches?

Disable the second factor, recompute the code from its shared secret, or drive a real device. Each has a domain where it is the right choice, and that domain comes down to one question: is there a secret that you hold?

All three, with their domain:

  • disable: simple and free, whenever the sign-in journey is out of the test's scope
  • recompute: a few lines of library, whenever you hold the shared secret
  • drive a real device: the only route when no secret can be obtained

The third case covers approval requests and sovereign identity apps. Why there is nothing to compute is explained in « automating 2FA without a shared secret », and the families of tools that handle it are compared in « which tool for testing 2FA on a real device ».

In practice

What does the call from a test look like?

An HTTP request, and nothing more. The test triggers the step, waits for the answer, and picks up where it left off. No SDK to embed in the application under test, no agent to install, and no API key to distribute through the integration pipeline.

The endpoints are detailed on the technical page, and the per-tool examples cover Cypress, Playwright, Robot Framework and JUnit. One end-to-end case is worked through in the LuxTrust guide.

And the security review

What do you tell the security team?

This is often the question that decides, and it does not come from the tester. It covers three points: where the data handled during a test goes, which secrets circulate, and what leaves the network. All three have a short, checkable answer.

In short: scenarios and their screenshots stay inside the box, on your network; no API key has to be distributed; and no internet connection is needed while the tests run.

The detail, including what this approach does NOT cover, is in « security, compliance and test data ».
