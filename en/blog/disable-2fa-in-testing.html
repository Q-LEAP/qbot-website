Should you disable 2FA in test environments?

Often yes, sometimes no, and the answer comes down to a single question: is the login journey part of what you are testing? This guide gives the three cases where disabling is the right call, the three where it is a risk, and how to decide in three minutes.

By Sylvain Perez, creator of Q-Bot. Updated 27 August 2026

What it changes

What exactly do you lose by disabling it?

You stop testing the journey your users take. A real sign-in goes through a second factor; the one in your tests does not. Everything that depends on that step falls outside coverage: the redirect after approval, session expiry, and what happens on refusal.

It is a trade-off, not a mistake. But you should know what you are setting aside.

  • the redirect after approval, often where a journey breaks
  • session expiry, which depends on when authentication happened
  • refusal and the return to the sign-in screen
  • repeated attempts and account lock-out

In short: you keep coverage of the application and lose coverage of its front door.

The three cases for yes

When is disabling it the right call?

When sign-in is not the subject of the test. A unit test, a UI test on an internal screen, a dataset built by script: in those three cases, clearing a strong authentication adds nothing to the coverage and only lengthens the run. An injected session is simpler than any tool.

Three situations where the answer is clear:

  • a unit or integration test that never goes through the interface
  • an internal screen reached with a session already opened by script
  • a dataset prepared through the API, where sign-in is only a prerequisite

In those cases an injected session or a service account without a second factor is simpler and faster than any tool.

The three cases for no

When is it a risk?

When sign-in IS the subject, when the test acts as a release gate, or when an auditor will ask for proof that the real journey ran. There, a test that bypasses the second factor does not prove what it is asked to prove.

The three expensive cases:

  • a regression suite on the sign-in journey itself
  • a release gate, where the test result is the decision
  • a regulated context, where you must show the real journey ran

The tell-tale sign: if nobody would agree to ship without a human having signed in once by hand, the test is not covering what you think it covers.

What the source says

What does Selenium's documentation recommend?

It devotes a page to the question and offers three routes: disable 2FA in the test environment, disable it for one account, or obtain the shared secret and recompute the code. All three are sound, and the definition it works from draws its own boundary.

The page is « Two Factor Authentication », under discouraged practices. It is an authoritative source, written by the project your team uses every day.

Read the definition it works from, though: it describes 2FA as a code received through an app, an SMS or an email. It covers neither requests to approve nor sovereign identity apps. Its third route, obtaining the shared secret, assumes a secret exists and that someone hands it to you.

That is exactly where its boundary lies, and it is the subject of the guide on automating without a shared secret.

The decision

How do you decide, in three questions?

Is the sign-in journey part of this test's scope? Do you hold the secret that lets you recompute the code? And does this test's result decide whether something ships or not? Three answers, and the choice is made without a meeting and without a proof of concept.

In order:

  • out of scope and nothing ships on it: disable, that is the simple choice
  • in scope and you hold the secret: recompute the code, a library is enough
  • in scope and no secret can be obtained: you need a real device

The third case covers approval requests and identity apps: the page covering the whole subject sets out the three approaches, and the LuxTrust guide gives a worked example.
