What does the manual authentication step cost?

Nobody can answer for you: the figure depends on your headcount and your runs. This guide gives the formula, the three costs that are systematically forgotten, and what the calculation does not say. You leave with your own number, not ours.

The calculation

How is the cost calculated, in hours?

Three numbers are enough: how many people this step ties up, how many minutes each of them loses per day, and how many working days the month holds. Their product gives minutes, minutes give hours, and hours convert into testing days, which is the unit you actually plan in.

The formula, as it stands:

  • testers involved × minutes lost per day × 21 working days
  • the result is in minutes: divided by 60, it gives hours
  • divided by 7, it gives testing days, the unit you plan in

The calculation sits on the demo page, with two sliders and the working shown under the result. A large number without its working is an advertising claim; with its working, it is a measurement you can argue with.

What gets forgotten

What are the three costs people forget?

The recovery time after an interruption, which appears in no timesheet. Night availability, which is not paid at daytime rates. And abandoned runs, whose cost is not the time spent but the information nobody ever got. The third one is the heaviest and the hardest to price.

All three, in the order in which they grow:

  • recovery after interruption: approving a code takes ten seconds, picking the thread back up takes longer
  • night availability: being on call does not compare to a daytime hour
  • abandoned runs: the cost is not the time spent, it is the defect nobody saw

The third is the heaviest and the hardest to cost. The symptom, though, is visible in the morning report: « why your night runs stop at login ».

On your side

How do you measure it this week?

With no tool and no meeting: for five days, record how many manual approvals your team performs and how many scenarios fail at that same step. Two columns in a spreadsheet are enough, and the result is hard to argue with.

The protocol, as it fits on the back of an envelope:

  • one row per day, two columns: approvals done by hand and scenarios failing at that step
  • five days, not one: a Monday does not look like the Thursday at the end of a sprint
  • note separately the runs relaunched the next morning

The record serves twice: it gives you the number, and it shows management where the time goes.

The limits of the calculation

What does this calculation not say?

It does not say what a solution costs, it says what the problem costs. Turning hours into money would need your hourly rate, which we do not have and will not invent. Putting the two together is your calculation, with your numbers.

Two points that make this calculation defensible:

  • it returns a result in hours and days, never in money: the hourly rate is yours
  • it assumes no gain on our side: it is your current cost, not a promised saving

What automating this step actually changes is described on the reference page, and pricing is given in a demo, not in a guide.
